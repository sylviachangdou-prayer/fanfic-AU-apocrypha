Use this guide for A Song of Ice and Fire, Game of Thrones, Fire and Blood, House of the Dragon, or an original low-magic dynastic setting. Read [source-grounding.md](source-grounding.md) first for named canon.

## 1. Select continuity and date

Record:

- Main-novel continuity and last completed event included.
- Fire and Blood or other historical-book continuity.
- Game of Thrones television continuity.
- House of the Dragon television continuity.
- Accepted companion material and maps.
- Explicit hybrid rules.

Fix the date, monarch, disputed claims, living dragons, current titles, marriages, betrothals, wards, hostages, known parentage, current wars, held castles, and who knows each secret. Book, television, and pseudo-historical narration can differ materially.

## 2. Preserve the political engine

The setting works through:

- Personal relationships carrying public law and military obligation.
- Claims that are legal stories backed by witnesses, force, archives, and repetition.
- Great rulers depending on local lords, households, harvests, roads, ships, and credit.
- Family love inseparable from succession, marriage, wardship, and hostage logic.
- Information moving slowly while rumor, prejudice, and self-interest move quickly.
- Violence solving an immediate dispute while creating feuds, vacancies, debts, and legitimacy problems.
- Magic entering a political world whose actors possess incomplete and contradictory models of it.

Do not equate seriousness with random cruelty. Consequence, constituency, and limited knowledge create the tone.

## 3. Power architecture

### Crown and central court

- Monarch: patronage, appointments, judgment, symbolic legitimacy, and dependence on coalition.
- Hand and Small Council: administration divided among finance, ships, law, intelligence, scholarship, and military advice; each office has its own information and clients.
- Kingsguard or equivalent: protection, prestige, oath conflict, and proximity to private life.
- Royal household: chamber, kitchen, stable, servants, pages, guards, companions, and access control.

### Territorial power

- Great houses: regional legitimacy, castles, marriages, fleets, food, and vassal networks.
- Bannermen and landed knights: service is negotiated through kinship, fear, interest, custom, and local rivalry.
- Towns, ports, merchants, guild-like interests, and smallfolk: taxation, supply, disease, labor, and rumor.
- Castles: households and logistics hubs, not empty backdrops for nobles.

### Parallel institutions

- Faith: marriage, legitimacy, public morality, sanctuary, ritual, and mass support.
- Citadel and maesters: medicine, letters, education, records, counsel, and institutional bias.
- Night's Watch or frontier bodies: oath, exile, supply, recruitment, and neglected warning.
- Free Cities, banks, mercenary companies, pirates, and foreign courts: credit, trade, exile, ships, and alternative legitimacy.

No institution is one mind. Identify factions, patrons, regional loyalties, and succession inside each.

## 4. Kinship, marriage, and legitimacy

Track:

- Birth order, sex, legitimacy, acknowledgment, disputed parentage, and customary precedent.
- Marriage contract, dowry, dower-like support, inheritance expectation, consummation claims, annulment risks, witnesses, and alliance obligations.
- Wardship as education, alliance, surveillance, affection, and hostage power.
- Fostering, sworn brotherhood, household service, and informal kinship.
- Incest rules, exceptional dynastic practice, religious tolerance, and political propaganda.
- Names, titles, styles, seals, heraldry, and who may speak for a house.

A claim needs a coalition. Genealogy alone does not create power; it supplies language through which soldiers, priests, lords, creditors, and towns justify a choice.

## 5. Information, travel, and war

- Track roads, ships, weather, mountains, rivers, distance, horses, baggage, and rest.
- Ravens transmit selected written messages between equipped locations; they do not create universal instant communication.
- Separate event time, report time, decision time, and arrival time.
- Armies require muster, food, fodder, carts, smiths, healers, camp labor, roads, and political permission.
- Campaign seasons compete with planting and harvest. A lord may lose military capacity by keeping people away from land.
- Sieges turn on supply, disease, engineering, relief, betrayal, and time more often than heroic assault.
- Dragons or other overwhelming weapons change formation, fortification, legitimacy, and terror, but still require riders, information, rest, feeding, and political aftermath.

Battle outcomes create wounded heirs, unpaid troops, empty offices, burned grain, refugees, contested credit, and stories about blame.

## 6. Household and material life

Use:

- Seating, precedence, clothing, mourning, gifts, food, cups, bedding, baths, chamber pots, heating, candles, and privacy.
- Wet wool, leather, horse care, road mud, salt, grain, ale, preserved food, childbirth, infection, scars, and chronic pain.
- Servants, guards, squires, ladies, stewards, cooks, septons, fools, singers, and maesters as information networks with their own aims.
- Accounts, inventories, keys, seals, letters, guest lists, kitchens, stable capacity, and room assignment as political tools.

Describe luxury comparatively: who has fresh fruit in winter, a private fire, glass, fine cloth, enough horses, imported spice, or the right to make others wait.

## 7. Magic, religion, and uncertain knowledge

For each viewpoint distinguish:

- What they have witnessed.
- What their culture teaches.
- What political interest encourages them to claim.
- What the reader may know from another continuity.

Prophecy should alter choices through interpretation and fear, not function as a clean outline. Dreams, dragons, resurrection, warging, shadow magic, old gods, red priests, and northern threats require source-specific verification. Do not let every courtier possess the reader's supernatural certainty.

Religion supplies ritual, law, community, legitimacy, fear, charity, and organized dissent. It is not a single level of personal belief.

## 8. Character-anchor cautions

Use the user's supplied anchors first. For common figures:

- Rhaenyra should not collapse into a generic modern heroine or a single claim. Track appetite for recognition, dynastic entitlement, intimate attachments, motherhood, anger, political learning, and how source continuity changes her choices.
- Jaime should not collapse into flippant beauty, redeemed knight, or incest alone. Track wit, shame, bodily identity, conflicting oaths, violence, family attachment, self-deception, and the gap between reputation and private judgment.
- Tywin should not collapse into an emotionless genius. Track dynasty as public project, administrative competence, punitive spectacle, class contempt, hypocrisy, children as extensions of legacy, and strategic errors produced by humiliation and control.

Dialogue distinction comes from status and defense: who jokes upward, repeats a phrase as warning, names law precisely, refuses a title, uses family history as a weapon, or hides fear behind boredom.

## 9. AU translation

- A noble house may become a coven, corporation, orbital dynasty, magical family, or assassin agency only if inheritance, dependents, reputation, and coercive resources translate.
- Dragons may translate into monopoly weapons, transport access, supernatural lineage, or strategic intelligence, but their unequal political effect must remain.
- Wardship may become academy sponsorship, corporate guardianship, therapeutic custody, hostage exchange, or mission mentorship.
- The Small Council may become a board, coven court, command staff, or ministry cabinet with divided portfolios.

Do not retain medieval titles after their material function disappears. Conversely, do not insert modern equality, privacy, policing, or bureaucracy without showing the institution that supports it.

## 10. Set pieces and audit

Strong set pieces include succession councils, royal weddings, births with disputed evidence, funerals, trials, feasts, tourneys, sieges, naval crossings, dragon confrontations, public oaths, bread riots, castle evacuations, betrayals during guest protection, or a battle whose delayed message changes another front.

Join the large event to a private objective: secure a witness, protect a child, control a door, deliver a letter, prevent a name from being spoken, force an oath, or reach a dragon. Track seating, entrances, banners, crowd layers, guards, sound, fire, animals, weather, and escape routes.

Audit:

1. Is the continuity explicit?
2. Does every claim have law, witness, archive, coalition, or force behind it?
3. Are crown, house, faith, Citadel, household, and foreign interests distinguishable?
4. Do travel and information take time?
5. Does violence change supply, succession, reputation, and allegiance?
6. Do characters speak and judge from their own historical position?
7. Does the spectacle leave administrative and emotional aftermath?
