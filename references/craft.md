## Scene function

Every scene needs at least two of the following and should usually contain all four:

- A character wants a concrete near-term outcome.
- Another person, rule, material fact, or internal contradiction resists it.
- Someone chooses rather than merely receives information.
- The choice changes access, risk, trust, knowledge, obligation, or available options.

An objective may be negative. A scene whose whole aim is to not do something — not drink, not answer, not look, not leave — takes its pressure from restraint rather than pursuit. Build it from the same parts: an escalating temptation, a specific cost for failing, a witness who must not notice, and a decision at the end about whether to stay in the room at all.

Every unit runs two lines at once: what is happening, and what is happening between these people. A unit that moves only the situation is a report. A unit that moves only the feeling is a mood piece. Both lines need their own conflict and their own high point inside the unit, at any length.

Bind the two so they load each other. The strongest form is mutual damage: the move that solves the situation costs something in the relationship, or the move that protects the relationship makes the situation worse. Two lines that never touch produce two thin stories instead of one.

The relationship line does not require the other party on the page. It can run entirely through an absence: an unexplained gap, an unanswered call, a habit that changed, a ration that stopped being drawn. Keeping the partner offstage through an opening is a deliberate move, not a missing scene. The tightest construction hands both lines the same fact, so that the discrepancy threatening a character in public is also the evidence of who they have been with.

Enter where the pressure becomes specific. Leave after the consequence becomes legible. An opening may use action, ritual, voice, an anomaly, a transaction, or a quiet disturbance; do not default mechanically to mid-action.

## Causality and pacing

- Build the next event from a prior choice or neglected consequence. Avoid coincidence as the main bridge between scenes.
- Alternate compression and expansion. Spend words where perception, choice, or relationship changes; compress routine travel and repeated procedure.
- Give major turns an aftereffect. A revelation without changed behavior is trivia.

Set the ratio before writing the scene. Most of what happens should compress into a clause; one or two moments get full space. A scene that gives every event the same treatment is a report, and it reads flat however good the sentences are.

Spend the expansion on environment, objects, and bodies rather than on events. What a room smells like, what someone is doing with their hands, what the light is doing to a surface — these hold a reader inside a scene. A sequence of narrated actions only moves them through it.

Every stretch of environment must do at least one job: set a pressure the characters work against, leak plot information the viewpoint has not registered, expose what this viewpoint selects and therefore who they are, or place an object a later scene will need. Environment doing none of these is scenery, and should be cut — but cutting all of it and keeping only events produces the flat report again.

Withhold at the level of cause, never at the level of sense. Do not explain why a character does what they do, what a look meant, what the relationship is, or how two facts connect. Do render what is physically present in full. The default failure runs the opposite way: generic sensory detail with every causal link spelled out.

This licenses no vagueness about what a thing is. Objects, names, and grammatical objects stay explicit — a document has a specific name, a verb keeps its object. Withhold interpretation, never identity.
- Let at least one resource, option, relationship, status, belief, or bodily capacity become harder to recover.
- Include quiet only when it carries appetite, work, discomfort, humor, resentment, or preparation. "Rest scenes" are still scenes.

## Long-arc architecture

Build a long story as connected pressure, not a sequence of locally exciting episodes.

- Establish a promise, a material or institutional conflict, a relationship imbalance, and at least one concealed causal fact.
- Widen the consequences through choices that recruit more people, systems, territory, debt, or public attention.
- Create an inversion that changes the meaning of earlier success, loyalty, evidence, or identity without invalidating what the reader saw.
- Converge independent character and institutional lines at the climax. Each line should arrive through its own cause.
- Make the climax demand a choice prepared by prior costs, not merely a stronger opponent.
- Give aftermath enough space to show altered bodies, organizations, relationships, resources, and public stories.

Use an escalation ladder across different dimensions: access, time, reputation, bodily capacity, institutional response, relationship exposure, and scale. Repeating physical danger at greater volume is not escalation.

Plant setups as usable things: a rule, skill, object, promise, misunderstanding, route, witness, debt, or organizational dependency. A payoff should transform the setup's meaning or cost, not simply mention it again.

## Character construction

Define major characters through vectors rather than labels:

- What they want now.
- What they protect without admitting it.
- What leverage they possess.
- What they consistently misread.
- What behavior becomes likely under shame, hunger, fatigue, fear, or authority.
- Which boundary they expect never to cross.

Give supporting characters an aim that exists outside the protagonist. At least one supporting character should be correct when the protagonist is wrong, and at least one consequential choice should originate outside the protagonist. Do not turn an ensemble into a panel that validates the lead.

No unit may contain only its principals. A two-hander still owes the reader a populated world, and the cost is usually a clause rather than a scene. Use three levels of presence:

- **Absent**: someone who cannot be reached, has not answered, is being deliberately not told, or is expected and does not come.
- **Quoted**: a third party's judgment, prediction, standing joke, warning, or rule living inside the principals' dialogue.
- **Present**: a character who arrives with an aim of their own and does not exist to witness the principals.

Use at least one level in every unit. At least one third party should hold an opinion of the principals that differs from their opinion of themselves.

Build an ensemble matrix with different combinations of competence, social leverage, risk tolerance, moral trigger, private concern, speaking behavior, and knowledge. If two characters occupy the same function, force them to want different outcomes from it.

For fanfiction, translate immutable character anchors into the AU. Preserve decision logic, defensive habits, values, and relational asymmetries; jobs, powers, costumes, and factions may change.

## Point of view

- Use one controlling consciousness per scene unless the requested form deliberately uses omniscience.
- Restrict description to what this viewpoint would notice, name, misunderstand, or avoid.
- Separate observation from inference. Let an inference remain wrong when the character lacks corrective evidence.
- Track knowledge states. No character may react to information they have not acquired.
- Control psychic distance deliberately. Do not slide from bodily immediacy to detached authorial analysis by accident.

## Worldbuilding in motion

- Reveal rules through attempted use, failure, transaction, punishment, maintenance, or unequal access.
- Prefer operational detail over explanatory history: who stamps the permit, what the filter smells like when spent, which oath wording transfers land, how long a message takes.
- Test every rule with trigger, cost, limit, exception, and evidence available to characters.
- Let institutions contain competing offices and incentives. "The government," "the corporation," and "the church" are not single minds.
- Make logistics visible when they constrain action: travel time, food, sleep, fuel, weather, communications, medicine, maintenance, labor, and disposal.

## Dialogue and subtext

- Give each speaker a distinct syntax, tolerance for directness, preferred evasions, vocabulary, and relationship to silence.
- Allow partial answers, tactical misunderstanding, interruption, repetition, failed jokes, and badly timed honesty.
- Do not use dialogue to recite shared knowledge.
- Put exposition under pressure: a lie at inspection, a lesson with a practical test, a negotiation, a diagnosis, a ritual, or an argument over procedure.
- Run a sustained misreading. Let one party misinterpret the other in a single consistent direction across a whole exchange, so every line carries a visible gap between what is heard and what is happening. The reader holding both readings at once is the engine of comedy and of dread alike; only the stakes decide which.
- Let body and task contradict speech. A character may clean a weapon, sort pills, or fasten someone else's coat while refusing the emotional subject.

## Register range and rhetorical risk

Prose that only avoids errors reads as competent and dead. Any unit above flash length must land at least two of the following three. A chapter that delivers one register has failed regardless of how clean it is.

- **A laugh.** A joke that belongs to a character and costs them something: badly timed, defensive, aimed at the wrong person, or left unacknowledged. Comedy comes from status, mistiming, and misplaced confidence. A wry narrator is not a joke.
- **A held breath.** A stretch where the reader and the viewpoint hold different information and the gap is about to close. Tension is an information problem before it is a danger problem.
- **A hit.** One moment aimed straight at the reader: a withheld thing finally given, a kindness arriving too late, an ordinary object revealed to have been carried a long way. Earn it through prior restraint, then do not soften it afterward.

Treat every comparison as a characterization act. A doctor, a smuggler, and a bored aristocrat do not reach for the same image. A stiff metaphor is usually a safe one; it fits the mood and reveals nobody. Prefer a figure that is slightly too much for the moment and unmistakably belongs to one mind over a tasteful one that belongs to no one.

One device carries most of the comedy available to close third: the standalone aside. A one-line paragraph in which the viewpoint judges their own situation, sitting outside the action and doing no narrative work whatsoever. It punctuates; it does not advance. A comic register can hold several per scene. A grim register holds one, and it lands harder for being alone. The aside must be the character's judgment rather than the author's — it dies the moment it becomes a joke the character is not making.

Let a joke fail. Let a metaphor be an overreach the character would defend. Let a line be too blunt because the speaker has run out of patience.

## Description and embodiment

- Establish spatial facts before complex action: distances, exits, elevation, light, sound, surfaces, obstacles, and where bodies are placed.
- Select sensory detail according to viewpoint expertise and current need. A medic, smuggler, child, and courtier do not scan the same room.
- Land light on something. A described light source with no object it falls on describes nothing; the object it picks out builds the room at the same time. One prop that fixes what kind of place this is beats three adjectives about the place.
- Make vagueness carry information. A deliberately unspecified detail must still smuggle a fact through: what a character habitually orders, how long they have been coming here, what they do not drink. Vagueness that carries nothing is not restraint, it is an empty slot.
- Avoid the default five-sense inventory. One precise material detail can carry more reality than a lyrical bundle.
- Use involuntary bodily response sparingly and specifically. Do not recycle clenched jaws, held breath, pounding hearts, and released breaths as emotional punctuation.

## Action and problem solving

- Establish a finite resource or clock and decrement it honestly.
- Let competence appear as attention followed by a choice whose value becomes clear later.
- Make expertise fail when its assumptions do not hold.
- Use errors of grip, footing, timing, visibility, communication, and fatigue. Avoid frictionless choreography.
- Resolve problems through established rules and costs, not a new ability introduced at the point of victory.

## Large set pieces

Design spectacle around a local objective inside a system losing control.

1. Establish a space the reader can sketch.
2. Layer participants by role: protagonists, authorities, workers, crowd, bystanders, opponents, and people who misunderstand the event.
3. Activate one setting-specific rule or institution.
4. Escalate through a causal cascade that changes routes, information, or objectives.
5. Force an irreversible character choice while the large event is still moving.
6. Show material and administrative aftermath: casualties, debris, records, blame, lost capacity, emergency rules, and changed alliances.

Use scale cues available to the viewpoint: time needed to cross, delayed sound, crowd waves, structural vibration, blocked sight lines, communication saturation, exhausted responders, or a familiar landmark made unusable. Do not replace geometry with adjectives such as vast, chaotic, or breathtaking.

## Intimacy and relationship arcs

- Track changes in access: who may touch, ask, refuse, witness weakness, use a name, enter a room, or hold an object.
- Let repeated objects or routines change meaning through context; do not explain the symbolism.
- Build attraction and attachment through attention, risk, inconvenience, misinterpretation, and choice.
- Keep consent legible. Power imbalance may create conflict, but the narration should not confuse coercion with proof of love.
- Allow declarations when earned, but do not use a declaration as a substitute for prior relational change.

Give the central relationship one unresolved question both parties avoid, in place before the story opens. Dramatize the avoidance before spending it: a topic they route around, a sentence one of them starts and drops, a silence that arrives after one specific word. The force of a reconciliation equals the avoidance the reader has watched, not the duration the narration claims. Stating that a line has gone unsaid for eleven years buys nothing if the reader has never once seen it go unsaid.

When the avoided question is structural and permanent — unequal lifespan, incompatible duty, an oath, a divided loyalty — do not solve it. Let the characters narrow its scope instead, trading an impossible promise for a smaller one that can actually be kept. A solved core asymmetry ends the story and leaves later installments running on residue.

## Serial propulsion

Each installment should give the reader both movement and residue:

- Deliver at least one promised pleasure of the premise: discovery, competence, danger, intimacy, political maneuver, horror, wonder, or revenge.
- Change the local situation rather than only tease future change.
- Carry forward a manageable number of open loops.
- End on the strongest honest pressure point, not a generic ominous sentence.
- Vary chapter architecture. Repeated hook-escalation-twist-cliffhanger rhythm becomes visible machinery.

## Final craft check

Confirm that the draft can answer these questions:

1. What did each major character want in this unit?
2. Which choice caused the largest consequence?
3. What became impossible, costly, or newly tempting?
4. Which world rule materially affected events?
5. What does the viewpoint misunderstand or refuse to name?
6. Why does the next scene need to exist?
